### 3. DRAFTED PARAGRAPH

Writing environments with background fact-checking and explicit accept or deny prompts can hold authors closer to shared oversight than full-text generation, yet these safeguards remain vulnerable to eroding authorial control. In Nandita Biswas Mellamphy's framework, such tools avoid pushing writers fully out of the loop while departing from sovereign mastery, positioning authors on the loop as "co-producers under specific but changing environmental conditions that are conceptualized as being both within and beyond human control" (Mellamphy 2021: 20). Inspecting flagged errors, weighing proposed revisions, and preserving personal wording while organizing rough ideas grants writers meaningful incremental agency even when complete control remains elusive. Still, Matt Carlson complicates this pragmatic view by asking "whether an increase in algorithmic judgment will lead to a decline in the authority of human judgment" (Carlson 2015: 429). When heavy editing introduces uniform machine cadence and automated verification projects objectivity, writers may defer to algorithmic revisions and drift toward passive approval. Luke Munn deepens this concern by showing that, although partial automation preserves human involvement, workers gradually "internalize the logic of the system and perform their activities in a way that is algorithmically recognized" (Munn 2022: 25). Continuous background intervention can thus condition writers to anticipate machine preferences before accepting prompts. Consequently, incremental oversight tools can slow departure from the loop, though genuine autonomy on the loop demands sustained resistance to algorithmic standardization.
